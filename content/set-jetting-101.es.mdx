Set-jetting is travel planned around the places you have already seen on a
screen. A skyline from the title sequence, the café where two characters fell
out, the cliff a dragon circled — you arrive already knowing the frame, and the
trip becomes a kind of treasure hunt. Done well, it is one of the most
rewarding ways to travel. Done badly, it clogs a quiet residential street with
strangers taking the same photo. Here is how to do it well.

## What set-jetting actually means

The term simply describes choosing a destination because something was filmed
there. It is not new — people have made pilgrimages to movie towns for decades —
but streaming has accelerated it. A single hit season can send a previously
sleepy town a year's worth of visitors in a summer. That is great for local
businesses and rough on local life, which is the tension every thoughtful
set-jetter has to hold.

The important thing to understand is that a "filming location" can mean two very
different places. Some sets are real, named spots you can walk straight into.
Others are clever composites: an exterior shot in one country, an interior built
on a soundstage in another, with a digital skyline stitched on top. Knowing
which is which before you go saves you a long detour to a car park.

## Do your homework before you go

A little research turns a vague idea into a real itinerary:

- **Confirm the spot is genuine.** Production blogs, regional film commissions
  and local tourism boards usually publish the real locations. If a place only
  appears on aggregator sites with no source, treat it as unconfirmed.
- **Check access and opening hours.** Plenty of iconic backdrops are private
  homes, working hotels, or ticketed monuments with timed entry. A castle that
  played a fortress might be open three days a week.
- **Look at the season.** The light that made a scene may only happen at a
  certain time of year or day. Sunrise and the hour after sunset also give you
  the thinnest crowds.
- **Map a cluster, not a pin.** Most screen towns have several spots within
  walking distance. Group them so you are tracing a route, not criss-crossing
  the city for one photo.

## Visit like a guest, not a location scout

The single rule that matters most: real people live and work at these places.

> A street is not a film set the day after the crew leaves. It goes back to
> being someone's doorstep.

Keep your voice down on residential squares. Do not block doorways, shopfronts
or narrow lanes for a photo. If a location is someone's home or a working
business, admire it from the public pavement and move on. Spend money locally —
the café on the corner, the museum that protects the ruin — so your visit leaves
something behind beyond footfall.

## A simple planning checklist

1. Pick one title and list the spots you actually want to see.
2. Confirm each one is a real, accessible location.
3. Book anything that needs a ticket or a table ahead of time.
4. Cluster the spots into a walkable or short-drive route.
5. Sort out where to stay nearby and how you will get around.
6. Go early, tread lightly, and leave the place better than a crowd would.

That last point is the whole game. The best set-jetting trips send you home with
the scene *and* a clear conscience — and they leave the location in good enough
shape that the next traveller, and the people who live there, are glad you came.
